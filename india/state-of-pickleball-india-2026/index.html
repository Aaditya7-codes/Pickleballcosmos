The State of Pickleball in India 2026

India clearly has a real pickleball boom. What it does not yet have is a reliable national census of players, courts or market value. The useful story is therefore not one spectacular growth number — it is what the strongest available evidence actually supports.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialPublished August 10, 2026
The short version: pickleball in India has moved beyond niche status. A nationally recognised federation structure, hundreds of ranking events and competitive players, professional franchise leagues, growing booking infrastructure and rapid court development all point in the same direction. But widely quoted player and court totals are not directly comparable, and no public source we found provides a transparent national census.

The first data problem: how many Indians actually play pickleball?

The most tempting question produces the least satisfying answer. There is no India-wide equivalent of the Sports & Fitness Industry Association participation study used in the United States, at least not one publicly available with a comparable sampling methodology.

That means figures describing “players” can refer to very different populations: federation members, tournament entrants, active recreational players, occasional players, app users or people reached by affiliated state bodies.

The Indian Pickleball Association currently says its ecosystem covers 50,000+ players, 500+ professional players and 27 states. Its tournament material separately refers to 50,000+ amateur players and 100+ state-ranking tournaments. Those are meaningful federation indicators, but they should not automatically be converted into a claim that India has exactly 50,000 total pickleball participants.

Cosmos position: we do not currently think the public evidence supports a precise national participation figure. “At least tens of thousands of organised or federation-reported players, with a larger recreational population likely” is more defensible than pretending one headline number is a census.

The second data problem: court counts are even messier

Court numbers are frequently repeated in Indian media, but the underlying definitions are rarely clear. A dedicated pickleball court is not the same thing as a badminton or tennis court temporarily lined for pickleball. A bookable commercial venue is not the same thing as a private residential court. And a “location” can contain one court or ten.

For example, a January 2025 Economic Times report on the launch of the World Pickleball League cited nearly 1,000 courts in India alongside more than 50,000 players in 2024. Other later industry estimates are higher. Without a transparent venue-level database, those totals cannot be independently reconciled.

What is easier to verify is the direction of travel: new dedicated facilities are appearing in major metros, residential developments, hotels and social-sports clubs, while booking platforms are increasingly treating racquet-sport inventory as a standalone category.

This is why Pickleball Cosmos will build its India court coverage from the bottom up: identifiable venues, court counts, city, booking method, price and last-verified date — rather than copying a national total whose denominator cannot be inspected.

India's competitive ecosystem has become substantial

Competition is one of the clearest signs that Indian pickleball has entered a new phase. The IPA tournament calendar lists events across cities including Greater Noida, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Surat, Jaipur, Guwahati, Bhopal, Udaipur and others. Published 2025 examples range from smaller PWR 50 events to the GoRally Open in Bengaluru, which listed 380 participants and ₹10 lakh in prize money.

The inaugural Indian Pickleball League in December 2025 added a different layer. Launched by The Times Group and sanctioned by the IPA, it debuted at KD Jadhav Indoor Hall in New Delhi with city franchises including Mumbai, Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Chennai and Gurgaon, with Lucknow later appearing in the competition. Adani joined as a major commercial partner. Mumbai Smashers ultimately beat Hyderabad Royals in the final.

India had already hosted the World Pickleball League earlier in 2025, with Sony Sports Network taking television rights. Taken together, these developments matter more than celebrity photos or “fastest-growing sport” slogans: they show broadcasters, sponsors, venue operators and franchise owners allocating real resources to the category.

Governance is more complicated than a single federation label

The Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports recognised the Indian Pickleball Association (IPA) as a National Sports Federation in April 2025. A February 2026 Delhi High Court judgment rejected a challenge by the older All India Pickleball Association (AIPA) to that recognition.

But the governance story did not end there. AIPA and another body appealed. On April 7, 2026, a Division Bench directed that when the government considered renewal of IPA's recognition, the rival applicants should also be allowed written submissions and a personal hearing. The court did not stay IPA's recognition at that hearing. Proceedings were subsequently adjourned in May.

That distinction matters. Saying simply “there is one uncontested governing body” would flatten an active institutional dispute. As of this report, IPA continues to operate the ranking and tournament ecosystem it describes as nationally recognised, while the recognition process has also been the subject of ongoing litigation and review.

Pickleball's Indian history predates the current boom

The sport did not arrive in India in 2024. The All India Pickleball Association says Sunil Valavalkar introduced pickleball to India in 2008 and founded AIPA that year. The International Federation of Pickleball also records AIPA's 2015 affiliation and notes that the organisation ran national tournaments, Federation Cups, Indian Opens and the 2022 Bainbridge Cup in India.

The important distinction is between introduction and commercial acceleration. Indian pickleball has an organisational history of more than a decade, but the explosion in urban venues, social play, leagues, booking platforms and lifestyle positioning is much more recent.

Why the sport fits urban India

The commercial logic is fairly straightforward. A pickleball court requires less playing area than a tennis court, doubles is inherently social, the learning curve is comparatively gentle, and games fit comfortably into bookable urban time slots. Those characteristics suit dense metro markets where consumers already pay for badminton, football turf, padel and other hourly recreational sports.

Recent NDTV reporting put urban pickleball bookings at roughly ₹600 to ₹1,500 per hour. That is not a national tariff — premium venues, peak hours and city economics can push pricing in either direction — but it shows why operators can view pickleball as a monetisable court product rather than simply a club amenity.

The sport is also appearing in settings that reveal its target audience: premium residential developments, corporate wellness programmes, clubs and resorts. That may help explain why pickleball currently feels disproportionately visible among urban professionals even though a robust national demographic study does not yet exist.

What about the ₹750 crore market forecast?

A widely circulated 2026 figure puts India's pickleball market at roughly ₹750 crore by 2030. It is useful as evidence that commercial participants expect growth; it should not be mistaken for an audited present-day market size.

Market forecasts are especially sensitive to what is included: paddles and balls, court construction, hourly bookings, coaching, tournaments, sponsorship, media rights, leagues, software and real estate can all be counted differently.

For now, Pickleball Cosmos will treat such forecasts as industry estimates, not measured facts. A more useful future project is to construct the market from observable components: venue inventory, booking prices, utilisation, equipment sales, tournament prize pools and sponsorship activity.

Where the strongest Indian markets appear to be

Public tournament calendars, venue launches and league franchises repeatedly point to a cluster of major urban markets rather than evenly distributed adoption. Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR and Hyderabad appear especially important, with significant activity also visible in Gujarat, Pune, Jaipur, Goa and other cities.

We are deliberately not publishing a “top 10 pickleball cities” ranking yet. A ranking without a consistent venue dataset would convert visibility into false precision. The next stage of the India Desk is to build that dataset.

What would prove that the boom is durable?

Celebrity participation and new court launches are easy to photograph. Durable sports markets require deeper signals. Over the next several years, we would watch five things:

  • Repeat participation: whether occasional urban trial converts into regular weekly play.
  • Court utilisation: whether new facilities sustain bookings after launch.
  • Youth pathways: school, junior and academy participation rather than only adult social play.
  • Competitive depth: larger fields, more ranked players and stable tournament calendars outside the biggest metros.
  • Commercial durability: equipment sales, sponsorship and leagues that survive beyond initial launch capital.

On those measures, India is promising but still early. That is precisely why good measurement matters now.

What we are building next

This report is the starting point, not a declaration that the data problem is solved. Pickleball Cosmos intends to build a verifiable India court tracker, beginning with major cities, and to separate venue-level observations from federation claims and industry estimates.

That will allow future editions of this report to answer harder questions with original evidence: how many bookable venues exist, which cities are adding courts fastest, what players actually pay, and where the sport is expanding beyond its current urban core.
