The History of Pickleball

Pickleball began as an improvised family game in Washington state in 1965. Six decades later, SFIA counted 24.3 million U.S. participants in 2025. What made that change possible was not a sudden invention, but decades of rules, local organizers, dedicated courts and institutions.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 11, 2026
The short history: Joel Pritchard, Bill Bell and Barney McCallum developed the game on Bainbridge Island in 1965. A dedicated court followed in 1967, the first known tournament in 1976, a national association and rulebook in 1984, and play in all 50 states by 1990. The modern acceleration came decades later: U.S. participation rose from 4.2 million in 2020 to 24.3 million in 2025.

Before the boom: a game becomes a national sport

1965: the game begins on Bainbridge Island

USA Pickleball traces the sport to the summer of 1965, when Washington congressman Joel Pritchard and businessman Bill Bell returned to Pritchard's Bainbridge Island home and improvised a game for their families. They used a perforated plastic ball, table-tennis paddles and an existing badminton court.

Barney McCallum, a nearby friend of the Pritchards, soon joined them and helped develop more durable paddles and the early rules. Pickleball was therefore not designed in one formal meeting. It evolved through repeated backyard play as the founders adjusted equipment and rules to make the game work.

Where did the name “pickleball” come from?

The most repeated origin story says the sport was named after the Pritchard family's dog, Pickles. USA Pickleball's own historical investigation supports a different sequence.

According to the Pritchard family account published by USA Pickleball, Joan Pritchard suggested “Pickle Ball” in 1965 as a reference to the “pickle boat” in crew — a boat assembled from leftover rowers. The family's dog Pickles arrived in 1968, three years after the game had already been named.

Cosmos treatment: the dog story is culturally famous, but the chronology published by USA Pickleball supports the pickle-boat explanation. Pickles appears to have been named after the game, not the other way around.

1967: the first permanent pickleball court

USA Pickleball's historical timeline places the first permanent pickleball court in 1967 at the Pritchard property. That is an important transition: the game had moved from improvising on an existing badminton setup toward having infrastructure built specifically for pickleball.

1976: the first known tournament

The first known pickleball tournament was held in Tukwila, Washington, in 1976. USA Pickleball notes that many participants were college tennis players with limited pickleball experience, a useful snapshot of how small the sport still was.

Competitive pickleball existed, but there was not yet the national tournament ecosystem, rating infrastructure or professional circuit associated with the modern game.

1984: national organization and the first rulebook

In 1984, the United States Amateur Pickleball Association was organized. USA Pickleball states that the first official rulebook was also published that year.

The importance of this milestone is larger than the name of the organization. A backyard game becomes much easier to scale when players in different places can use a common rule set, equipment framework and organizing body.

USA Pickleball's current rulebook still identifies 1984 as the year the Official Rulebook was first published.

1990: played in all 50 states

USA Pickleball's historical record says pickleball was being played in all 50 U.S. states by 1990. That does not mean participation was yet large by today's standards, but it establishes that the sport had already become geographically national well before its modern boom.

1990s–2000s: infrastructure before the explosion

For much of its history, pickleball expanded through local clubs, recreation centers, retirement communities, municipal courts, schools and volunteer organizers rather than through mass media or a large professional sports machine.

This period matters because the post-2020 boom did not emerge from zero. The sport already had decades of accumulated rules, local communities, dedicated courts, tournaments and institutional knowledge.

2015: pickleball passes two million U.S. participants

USA Pickleball's historical timeline cites SFIA participation at just over two million U.S. players in 2015. In hindsight, that figure marks the beginning of a period in which pickleball shifted from a niche recreation story into a significant participation-sports market.

The boom inherited an older system

2020: 4.2 million players — and the curve changes

SFIA counted about 4.2 million U.S. pickleball participants in 2020. That number is now useful mostly as a baseline, because what happened next was far faster than the sport's earlier growth.

By 2022, SFIA reported 8.9 million participants. The count rose to 13.6 million in 2023, 19.8 million in 2024 and 24.3 million in 2025.

The professional game becomes a real ecosystem

The participation boom coincided with a much more visible professional game. The Professional Pickleball Association's official tournament archive records a substantial schedule by 2020, including events such as the Mesa Grand Slam, The Showcase, Atlanta Open, Texas Open and PPA Championships.

The significance is not merely that professionals were playing for titles. A modern professional ecosystem creates recurring stars, sponsorship inventory, broadcast content, equipment marketing and a pathway for fans to follow pickleball as a spectator product rather than only a recreation.

Professional pickleball has since continued to evolve through tour structures, team competition and changing commercial ownership. Because those structures can change quickly, Pickleball Cosmos treats the current business of professional pickleball separately from the stable historical timeline.

2025: 82,613 known courts and more than 100,000 USA Pickleball members

Participation growth required infrastructure. USA Pickleball's 2025 Annual Growth Report lists 82,613 known courts across 18,258 locations in the Pickleheads database and 104,828 USA Pickleball members.

Those are not interchangeable measures of the size of the sport. A court database is not a perfect census of every playable court, and USA Pickleball membership is not the same thing as total participation. But together they show how much institutional and physical infrastructure now exists around the game.

2026: the sport is dealing with mature-sport problems

One sign of pickleball's evolution is the nature of the problems its governing body now has to solve. USA Pickleball's 2026 activity includes more sophisticated paddle compliance testing, field-testing programs, counterfeit-equipment concerns, expanded championship pathways and continued facility development.

Those issues are different from the challenges of a backyard game trying to establish basic rules. They are the problems of a large sport with manufacturers, sanctioned competition, commercial venues and millions of recreational participants.

The history is not simply “old people discovered a backyard game”

That caricature misses most of what made pickleball durable.

The sport survived for decades before becoming fashionable. It standardized early enough to travel, remained relatively accessible to new players, fit into existing recreation infrastructure, developed strong local communities, and later benefited from dedicated facilities, commercial investment and professional competition.

The remarkable part of the story is therefore not just the post-2020 growth rate. It is that a game developed by three families in 1965 had enough institutional depth to absorb that growth more than half a century later.

Bottom line

Pickleball's origin story is charming, but its longevity is more important than its novelty.

It took roughly half a century to move from Bainbridge Island to four million U.S. participants. It then added about twenty million more participants in five years.

The history of pickleball is therefore two stories at once: a slow institutional build beginning in 1965, followed by one of the sharpest modern participation accelerations in American sport.
