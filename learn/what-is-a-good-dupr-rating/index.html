What Is a Good DUPR Rating? 3.0 vs 3.5 vs 4.0 vs 4.5

There is no official DUPR cutoff for “good.” A 4.0 is a reasonable shorthand for strong, advanced competitive recreational play, but the better interpretation depends on your comparison group, format and—often ignored—how reliable the rating is.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialPublished August 12, 2026
The short answer: DUPR's own published bands place 3.00–3.99 in an intermediate range, 4.00–4.99 in an advanced range, and 5.00+ in elite/pro-level territory. But those broad labels are not percentiles, and a 4.0 with very low reliability is not the same kind of evidence as a 4.0 built from a deep, current match history.

DUPR itself groups 2.00–2.99 as beginner to early intermediate, 3.00–3.99 as intermediate, 4.00–4.99 as advanced, and 5.00–8.00 as elite/pro-level.

Those are broad bands. They are useful for orientation, but they do not tell you where a specific 3.72 or 4.18 sits in the national player distribution.

So—is a 4.0 DUPR good?

Yes, in ordinary recreational and amateur conversation, a reliable 4.0 DUPR is a strong rating. DUPR places the 4.00–4.99 band in its advanced category, and competitive structures regularly use 4.0-level groupings as a meaningful step above intermediate play.

But “good” is not a formal status conferred at 4.000. Nothing magical happens when a profile moves from 3.999 to 4.001. DUPR is continuous, not a series of certification exams.

The useful takeaway is that 4.0 is a recognizable competitive benchmark—not an official line separating good players from bad ones.

What about 3.5?

A 3.5 DUPR should not be dismissed as “average beginner” territory. DUPR's own broad scale places it inside the intermediate range, and USA Pickleball's separate descriptive skill framework labels 3.5 as Intermediate.

At that level, the game is no longer just about making contact and keeping score. USA Pickleball's descriptive material expects growing shot selection, strategy and rally management, although execution is not yet as consistent as at higher levels.

The important word there is separate: USA Pickleball's skill descriptions are not the DUPR algorithm. A 3.5 DUPR comes from results; a 3.5 descriptive skill level is based on observed proficiencies. They can be useful cross-checks, but they are not mathematically interchangeable.

Why we are not publishing fake DUPR percentiles

You will sometimes see claims such as “a 4.0 DUPR is top 10%” or “3.5 is the national average.” Those statements sound precise, but precision requires a current distribution dataset.

As of our August 12, 2026 review, Pickleball Cosmos did not find a current official public DUPR table that maps every rating to a national percentile across the full rated-player population. DUPR publishes player rankings and selected platform statistics, but that is not the same thing as a representative rating distribution.

So we will not invent a percentile or repeat a third-party one as though DUPR published it. If a reproducible current distribution becomes available, this page can add percentiles with the denominator, date and methodology visible.

Why this matters: a rating can be “strong” without us pretending to know that it places you at the 82nd, 91st or 96th percentile. Those are different claims requiring different evidence.

Reliability changes how seriously you should read the decimal

A DUPR profile contains another number that matters: the Reliability Score.

DUPR says Reliability runs from 1% to 100%, with 60% its recommended passing threshold and 100% considered fully reliable. The score reflects how much confidence the system has in the rating based on match history, recency and connectivity to other players.

That means two identical ratings can carry different evidentiary weight. A 4.02 built from a large, recent, varied match history is a more established estimate than a new 4.02 derived from very little data.

This does not mean the low-reliability player is secretly worse. It means the estimate is less settled.

Competition structure gives the number real-world context

DUPR-based competition often turns individual ratings into team or division limits. That provides a more useful benchmark than vague labels such as “pretty good.”

For example, DUPR's 2026 Minor League/college materials describe competition levels including:

Competition levelApproximate player average in cited DUPR format

DUPR 164.0 average
DUPR 184.5 average

That is useful because it shows 3.5, 4.0 and 4.5 are not arbitrary internet labels: they correspond to materially different competitive bands in DUPR-organized play.

How far is 5.0 from professional?

Five-point-zero is high-level pickleball, but it is not the top of DUPR's scale. DUPR extends to 8.000.

For perspective, DUPR's men's doubles rankings dated July 15, 2026 listed leading professional ratings around the high-6s to low-7s, with Ben Johns at 7.121 and several other leading players above 6.8.

That gap is useful context. “5.0+” can reasonably be called elite competitive territory in DUPR's broad classification, but professional-world-class performance occupies a much higher part of the numerical scale.

What is a good DUPR for a recreational player?

The most useful answer depends on where and why you play.

Your goalA better question than “Am I good?”
Balanced open playIs my rating reliable enough to put me with players who produce competitive games?
League / tournament entryWhich division does the organizer use, and what rating/reliability rules apply?
ImprovementIs my rating trending because my performance is improving, or because my early rating is still calibrating?
Comparison with friendsAre we comparing the same format—singles vs singles or doubles vs doubles—and similarly reliable ratings?

If the purpose of a rating is better competition, an accurate 3.6 is more useful than an inflated 4.0. DUPR itself exists to estimate level, not to award status.

Do not confuse DUPR with USA Pickleball Skill Level

This distinction is easy to miss because both systems use familiar numbers such as 3.0, 3.5 and 4.0.

DUPR is a results-based rating. It updates from match data through an algorithm.

USA Pickleball's Skill Level is a descriptive proficiency framework. Its current guidance explicitly says skill levels are not official ratings; they describe knowledge, consistency, shot execution and strategy.

So a page that simply copies USA Pickleball's 4.0 skill checklist and calls it “what a 4.0 DUPR means” is collapsing two different measurement systems.

The answer to remember

If you need one benchmark, a reliable 4.0 DUPR is a strong rating for an amateur/recreational player. DUPR itself places 4.00–4.99 in its advanced band.

But do not turn that into false precision. There is no official “good starts at exactly 4.000” rule, and we do not have a current official national percentile table that proves where each decimal sits among all players.

Use the number for what it is designed to do: estimate playing strength, place you in better-matched competition and show how your performance changes over time.
