Can You Call a Fault on Your Opponent in Pickleball?

Usually no. In player-called play, you may enforce only two opponent faults: a non-volley-zone fault and a service foot fault. For every other alleged fault, the player who committed it makes the final decision.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialChecked August 15, 2026
The ruling: call your own faults and your partner’s. You may call an opponent only for a kitchen/NVZ fault or a service foot fault. Call promptly; if teams disagree on either permitted opponent call, replay the rally.

The rule is narrower than most players think

Rule 9.B.2 expects players to call faults on themselves or their partner as soon as the fault is committed or detected. Rule 9.B.3 then creates the only player-called exception for an opponent: non-volley-zone faults and service foot faults.

That means the normal answer to “Can I call that on them?” is no—even when you believe the opponent carried the ball, made an illegal double hit, touched the net or struck the ball before it crossed the plane. Rule 9.B.4 allows you to mention the specific issue after the rally, but not to enforce it. The final decision belongs to the player alleged to have committed the fault.

Timing: call the two permitted opponent faults promptly

A player should call a service foot fault or non-volley-zone fault as soon as it is committed or detected. The call must be made before the serve that begins the next rally. Waiting until a later point, or treating the call as leverage after the next serve, is too late.

A fault call produces a dead ball. The practical sequence is simple: stop play, identify the permitted fault, then resolve any disagreement before the next serve.

If teams disagree, replay—not debate

When teams disagree about a player-called service foot fault or non-volley-zone fault, the rally must be replayed. The rule does not ask players to negotiate which view was more persuasive.

Partner disagreement works differently. If the partners on the calling side disagree about a fault call, the decision goes to their opponents. The rule rewards a clear, unified call and prevents a team from taking a fault when one partner does not support it.

What “mention it” means for other faults

If you believe an opponent carried the ball, touched the net or committed another fault outside the two permitted categories, you may mention the specific fault after the rally. You cannot take the rally or demand a replay on that basis. The other player decides whether to call the fault on themselves.

This is not a loophole for silence. It is the self-officiating structure: the player closest to the alleged action has the responsibility to call it. The rule limits which opponent observations can change a result directly.

Officiated play is different

A referee identifies and calls rule violations. In tournament matches, the referee is specifically responsible for service foot faults, short serves and non-volley-zone faults. With line judges, they make line calls and foot-fault calls for their assigned line, while assisting the referee as requested.

Do not import the player-called limitation into an officiated match. The assigned officials—and the tournament procedure—control there.

Related rulings

This page is about who has authority to call the fault, not whether a particular action is a fault. For the underlying rules, see service and kitchen foot faults, kitchen momentum, carry and scoop, the net-plane ruling and line-call responsibility.

Bottom line

In player-called pickleball, enforce your own faults and your partner’s; enforce an opponent only for a service foot fault or non-volley-zone fault. Make either call promptly. If teams disagree, replay. For all other alleged opponent faults, state the issue after the rally and let the alleged player decide.
