Are Double Hits Legal in Pickleball? The 2026 Rule Explained

Yes. A ball may contact one player's paddle more than once and still be legal if the contacts occur during one continuous, single-direction stroke. The 2026 rule deliberately says more than once, so the same test can apply to a rare triple hit.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialPublished August 12, 2026
The rule in one sentence: one player may contact the ball multiple times during one stroke, provided that stroke remains continuous and moves in a single direction. A second stroke, a change in stroke direction, or contact by the player's partner makes it a fault.

The 2026 rule changed in a small but important way

USA Pickleball moved the double-hit rule from 2025 Rule 11.A to 2026 Rule 10.D. More importantly, it changed the wording from a ball being hit twice to a ball being hit more than once.

The reason is unusually specific: USA Pickleball's 2026 Rulebook Change Document says that, on rare occasions, a triple hit by the same player is possible and should be treated the same way as a double hit.

The wording that matters: it is the stroke, not a second swing

Rule 10.D does not give a player two attempts at the same ball. It allows multiple contacts that occur inside one legal stroke.

Imagine a fast defensive block where the ball catches the paddle face and then the edge during the same uninterrupted motion. That can satisfy the rule. By contrast, if a player contacts the ball, resets or redirects the paddle, and then contacts it again, the second action is no longer part of one continuous, single-direction stroke.

This distinction is more precise than the common recreational shorthand that “you can hit it twice if it was accidental.” Accident is not the governing test.

Intent does not decide whether the double hit is legal

Older explanations often say a legal double hit must be unintentional. That is outdated.

USA Pickleball removed the word “unintentional” from the double-hit rule in 2024. The same rules change removed “deliberately” from the carry rule. The stated reason was straightforward: referees should not have to determine a player's intent. The observable stroke and contact are what matter.

Do not use intent as the shortcut. The current question is whether one player made one continuous, single-direction stroke—not whether the player meant for the paddle to contact the ball twice.

“Single direction” refers to the stroke

Another common explanation says the ball must continue in exactly the same direction after both contacts. That is not how Rule 10.D is written. The rule requires the stroke to be continuous and in a single direction.

A dramatic change in the ball's path may be evidence that the player changed or restarted the stroke, but ball trajectory is not a substitute for the rulebook test. For a close ruling, evaluate the player's stroke rather than inventing a separate flight-path requirement.

Can both partners hit the ball?

No. The 2026 rulebook separates this into its own provision, Rule 10.D.2, Two-Player Hit. If a player and their partner both hit the ball in returning it, the team has committed a fault.

The contacts can be extremely close together. What matters is that two different players participated in returning the same ball. The legal multiple-contact exception belongs to one player.

Can a double hit happen on a serve?

The multiple-contact rule applies to legal play generally; it does not create an exemption from the serving rules. A serve still has to satisfy the applicable release, stroke, foot-position and placement requirements.

So a second paddle contact inside the same continuous, single-direction stroke is not automatically a fault merely because it occurred on a serve. But if the server makes a second stroke, changes direction to strike the ball again, or otherwise violates the serving rules, the serve is illegal.

Double hit versus carry: they are not the same rule

A legal multiple contact is still a stroke. A carry or catch involves the ball being controlled on the paddle rather than being struck through the qualifying motion.

This distinction matters because the removal of intent from the rules did not legalize carries. USA Pickleball's 2024 change made a carry/catch a fault regardless of whether the player deliberately caused it. A player therefore cannot defend an obvious catch-and-sling action by saying it happened in one motion.

The practical standard did not become looser. USA Pickleball clarified the rule's scope and structure: multiple contacts can be legal, but only within the same continuous, single-direction stroke by one player.

Three myths worth retiring

“Any double hit is a fault.”

False. Multiple contacts can be legal under Rule 10.D.

“It is legal only if it was accidental.”

Outdated. Intent was removed from the rule in 2024. Stroke mechanics control.

“The ball cannot change direction between contacts.”

That is an over-simplification. The current wording regulates the continuity and direction of the stroke. The ball's path can be evidence when judging what happened, but it is not the rule's standalone test.

The ruling to remember

Do not count paddle contacts. Judge the stroke.

Two—and, in rare circumstances, three or more—contacts by the same player can be legal if they happen during one continuous, single-direction stroke. Once the player makes a second stroke, changes the stroke's direction to strike the ball again, or the partner also hits the ball, it is a fault.
