Singles vs Doubles Pickleball Rules: What Actually Changes

Singles and doubles use the same 20 × 44-foot court and the same core rules for serving, the kitchen, the two-bounce rule, lines and faults. The meaningful rule differences are the score call, service sequence and how players establish their correct serving and receiving positions.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 10, 2026
The short version: singles is not played on a narrower court and it does not have a different kitchen. In traditional side-out scoring, singles uses a two-number score and one server per side; doubles uses a three-number score and normally gives both partners a service turn before side-out. The first service sequence of a doubles game is the exception.

What stays exactly the same

USA Pickleball’s rules summary states that pickleball may be played as singles or doubles and that the same size playing area and rules are used for both. That matters because several intuitive assumptions from tennis do not carry over.

The court does not get narrower for singles

Singles uses the entire pickleball court: 20 feet wide by 44 feet long. There are no singles sidelines inside the doubles sidelines. One player simply has to cover the same width that two players cover in doubles.

The kitchen rule is the same

The non-volley zone remains 7 feet deep on each side of the net. You may enter it to play a bounced ball, but you may not volley while touching the zone or its line, and volley momentum cannot carry you into it. None of that changes because only one player is on a side.

The two-bounce rule is the same

The receiver must let the serve bounce. The serving side must then let the return bounce. After one bounce has occurred on each side, either side may volley when otherwise legal. Singles does not remove or shorten that opening sequence.

The serve itself follows the same legal-service requirements

Singles and doubles use the same rules for legal volley serves and drop serves, foot position, ball release and diagonal placement. The difference is not how the paddle strikes the serve; it is how the correct server and service court are determined.

The biggest difference: how serving works

Singles: your score tells you where to serve

Traditional singles is simple to track. When the server’s score is even — 0, 2, 4, 6 and so on — the serve begins from the right/even service court. When the server’s score is odd, the serve begins from the left/odd service court.

If the server wins the rally, the server scores a point, switches sides and serves again. If the server loses the rally, service passes directly to the opponent. There is no partner waiting for a second service turn.

Doubles: both partners normally serve before side-out

After a side-out in traditional doubles, the team beginning its service sequence starts from the right-hand court. The first server continues serving, switching sides after each point scored, until the team loses a rally. The partner then becomes the second server and serves from the partner’s correct position.

When the second server loses a rally, the service sequence ends and the opponents take over. That is the side-out.

The opening doubles sequence is the exception

At the beginning of a new doubles game, the starting team does not get both service turns. Only one partner serves before the first side-out. Under traditional scoring, this is why the opening score is called 0–0–2: the opening server is treated as the second server for score-calling purposes.

Why the exception exists: without it, the team chosen to serve first would begin the game with two uninterrupted service opportunities while the receiving team had none. The opening sequence limits the first team to one server before normal doubles rotation begins.

The score call is different

Singles uses two numbers

A traditional singles score such as 6–4 means the server has 6 and the receiver has 4. There is no third number because there is no first-server/second-server distinction.

Doubles uses three numbers

A doubles score such as 6–4–1 means the serving team has 6, the receiving team has 4 and the team’s first server is serving. If the score is 6–4–2, the second server is serving.

The third number identifies the server’s place in that service sequence — it is not the player’s permanent identity for the entire game.

Correct position in singles

Singles positioning is governed by the server’s score. Even score means the server is on the right; odd means left. The receiver takes the diagonally opposite receiving position for that serve.

This makes singles easier to audit during a game: if the server says 7–5 but is standing on the right, something is wrong because 7 is odd.

Correct position in doubles

Doubles requires tracking two players on each side. Players do not simply choose whichever service box feels convenient. Their correct position flows from the score, the team’s starting positions and the service sequence.

A useful anchor from USA Pickleball’s traditional scoring summary is the team’s first server: when that team’s score is even, the player who was the team’s first server in the game will be in the right-side court when serving or receiving; when the team score is odd, that player will be on the left. Partners therefore switch sides when their team scores while serving, but not merely because possession of the serve changes.

Do singles players serve only to half the court?

The serve still goes diagonally into the correct service court, just as it does in doubles. Once the return is made, however, the full court is available for the rally. There is no singles alley to exclude and no rule requiring groundstrokes to stay crosscourt.

What changes strategically — but is not a rule

This distinction is often blurred in online explanations. Singles and doubles play very differently, but many of those differences are tactical rather than regulatory.

What about rally scoring in 2026?

The comparison above describes traditional side-out scoring, which remains the baseline system most players recognize. USA Pickleball also recognizes rally scoring provisionally in 2026, and sanctioned tournament directors may use it in permitted formats. Rally scoring awards a point after each rally and changes some service-sequence mechanics, especially in doubles.

That means a tournament using rally scoring should not be explained with the traditional “both doubles partners serve before side-out” shorthand. Always check the event’s scoring format before play. USA Pickleball continues to publish separate guidance for traditional side-out scoring and rally scoring.

Quick answers

Is a singles pickleball court smaller?

No. Singles and doubles use the same 20 × 44-foot court.

Is the kitchen smaller in singles?

No. The non-volley zone is unchanged.

Does singles use 0–0–2?

No. Traditional singles uses a two-number score because there is only one server on each side. The 0–0–2 opening call belongs to traditional doubles.

How do I know which side to serve from in singles?

Serve from the right when your score is even and from the left when it is odd.

Do both doubles players get to serve?

Under traditional side-out scoring, normally yes: both partners receive a service turn before side-out. The starting team’s first service sequence of the game is the exception — only one partner serves before the first side-out.

Are kitchen and two-bounce rules different in singles?

No. The core rally rules apply in both formats.
