Pickleball Faults: The Rules That End a Rally

A fault ends the rally, but “fault” is not one single rule. The 2026 rulebook places faults with the situations they govern: serving, rally play, the kitchen, paddle contact, the net and player contact. Start with the action, then use the specialist ruling where timing or an exception matters.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 15, 2026
The ruling: a called fault or a fault committed by a player creates a dead ball. Most faults occur while the ball is live. The important exception is a kitchen/NVZ momentum fault, which can be completed after the ball becomes dead.

Find the rule by the action—not by a generic fault list

The useful question is not merely “Was that a fault?” It is “What kind of action happened, and when?” A baseline contact belongs in the serving rules; post-volley movement belongs in the non-volley-zone rules; a paddle contact may be a legal multiple hit or a prohibited carry under different sections of the rulebook.

This is why the reference is built as a map. A broad summary can get you to the category, but it should not flatten a timing test or an exception that decides the rally.

Timing changes the answer

Most faults exist only while the ball is live. A ball that has already bounced twice is too late to return; touching the net while the ball remains live is a fault; and a player cannot strike the ball before it entirely crosses to that player’s end of the net.

The notable exception is kitchen momentum. A player may volley legally from outside the kitchen, but if the momentum from that volley carries the player—or something in contact with the player—into the non-volley zone, it remains a fault even after the ball becomes dead. That is a different test from simply standing in the kitchen to hit a bounced ball.

Player-called authority: the rule about the rule

In player-called play, call faults on yourself or your partner as soon as they are committed or detected. You may enforce only two types of opponent fault: a non-volley-zone fault and a service foot fault. If teams disagree about either permitted opponent call, replay the rally; if partners disagree, the decision favors the opponents.

For an alleged carry, illegal double hit, net touch or net-plane fault by an opponent, you may mention the specific issue after the rally, but you do not enforce it. The player alleged to have committed it makes the final fault decision. Read the complete fault-call authority ruling →

Officiated play has different responsibility

In an officiated match, the referee identifies and calls rule violations. The referee is specifically responsible for service foot faults, short serves and non-volley-zone faults; assigned line judges make line and foot-fault calls for their lines. The player-called limits are not a substitute for the tournament’s officiating procedure.

Three common shortcuts that fail

“Any double hit is a fault.”

Not necessarily. Multiple contacts by one player can be legal in one continuous, single-direction stroke. A carry is a different rule.

“You cannot enter the kitchen.”

Incorrect. The restriction is on volleying from the zone and on prohibited volley momentum, not ordinary entry to play a bounced ball.

“A line is always in.”

For ordinary rally shots, a ball touching a line is in. A serve touching the non-volley-zone line is short and therefore a fault. Read the line-rule distinction →
