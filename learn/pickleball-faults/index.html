Pickleball Faults: The Rules That End a Rally

A fault is a rule violation that ends the rally. The most common ones are simple: an illegal serve, a ball out or into the net, a two-bounce violation, a kitchen volley, a double bounce, touching the net while the ball is live, or being struck by the ball.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 15, 2026
The practical rule: if your side commits the fault, your side loses the rally. Under traditional side-out scoring, a receiving-team fault gives the serving team a point; a serving-team fault costs the serve, moving to the next server or a side out as applicable.

What counts as a fault?

USA Pickleball's rules summary defines a fault as an action that stops play because of a rule violation. The 2026 rulebook was reorganized so faults are generally placed alongside the rules they enforce instead of being collected in one stand-alone fault section.

That means an older article telling you to find “the complete fault list in Section 7” is using an outdated map of the rulebook.

1. Serving faults

Serving faults are rule-specific because legality depends on where the server stands, how the ball is released, how a volley serve is struck and where the ball lands.

Common examples include stepping on the baseline at contact, standing outside the permitted serving area, violating the volley-serve motion requirements, manipulating the release to add spin, or sending the ball somewhere other than the correct diagonal service court.

One important non-fault: a serve that clips the net and still clears the non-volley zone and lands in the correct service court is legal.

2. Violating the two-bounce rule

The receiver must let the serve bounce before returning it. The serving team must then let that return bounce before playing the third shot. Volleying either of those two required-bounce balls is a fault.

Full guide: The Two-Bounce Rule →

3. Volleying from the kitchen

The kitchen, formally the non-volley zone, creates one of pickleball's most misunderstood faults. Entering the zone is legal. Volleying while touching it is not.

The boundary line is part of the non-volley zone. Momentum also matters: if you volley legally from outside the zone but the action carries you into it afterward, the volley is still a fault.

Full guide: The Kitchen Rule →

4. Hitting the ball out

A ball that first lands outside the court is out and ends the rally. For ordinary rally shots, a ball touching a boundary line is in. The serve has a special line exception: the non-volley-zone line is short, so a serve touching that line is a fault.

This distinction matters because “a line is in” is broadly useful shorthand, but it is not complete for service placement.

Full guide: Pickleball Line Rules →

5. Hitting the ball into the net

If your shot fails to cross to the opponent's side and the rally dies in the net, your side loses the rally. Touching the net with the ball is not automatically a fault: a ball that clips the net and continues over may remain live.

6. Letting the ball bounce twice

For standard standing play, a player must return the ball before a second bounce on that player's side. Failing to do so is a fault.

This is different from the two-bounce rule, which requires one bounce on each side at the start of a rally. Adaptive and wheelchair rules can modify the permitted number of bounces.

7. Touching the net while the ball is live

If a player, the player's clothing or the player's paddle touches the net or net post while the ball is live, the contact can produce a fault. The timing matters: the restriction is tied to the live-ball period.

8. Being hit by the ball

After the serve, if a live ball strikes a player or something the player is wearing or carrying, the fault is generally on the player who was hit. There is a narrow paddle-hand exception: contact with a hand that is in contact with the paddle and below the wrist can leave the ball live. Serves have separate before-the-bounce contact rules.

A double hit is not automatically a fault

Multiple contacts can be legal when the ball strikes the paddle more than once during one player's continuous, single-direction stroke. If partners each contact the ball, or the contacts result from separate strokes, the result is different.

Full ruling: When a Pickleball Double Hit Is Legal →

9. Contact with a permanent object

A live ball can become dead when it contacts a permanent object. The 2026 changes clarified a scenario in which the ball has already bounced legally in a player's court: if that player then fails to return it before it contacts a permanent object, the fault is against the player who failed to make the return.

10. Stopping play when you are not entitled to

A valid hinder can justify stopping play and replaying the rally. An invalid stoppage can itself create a fault. The broader lesson is that fault, dead ball, hinder and replay are not interchangeable terms.

Who faults?Typical result under side-out scoring

The fault ends the rally; the scoring format determines how that rally result affects score and service.

Why the 2026 rulebook is easier to use

USA Pickleball reorganized the rulebook so related rules sit together and faults are placed with the rules to which they apply. For players, that means the best way to understand faults is by situation: serving faults with serving, kitchen faults with the non-volley zone, rally faults with rally play.
