Pickleball Line Calls: Who Makes the Call?

The receiving side calls its own lines. In a game without line judges, players make “out” calls for balls landing on their end of the court. In doubles, either partner may call it. If there is doubt, the ball is in; if partners disagree, the ball is in.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialChecked August 15, 2026
The ruling: call only what lands on your end. You need a clear view of space between ball and line to call out. Uncertainty favors the opponent; a partner conflict means in.

Whose responsibility is it?

USA Pickleball's 2026 line-call rule puts responsibility on the players for “out” calls on their own end of the court. That is the practical rule for ordinary recreational and non-line-judge play: the team receiving the ball decides whether it landed out.

This is not an invitation to call a close ball against the opponent. The rule's ethical standard is built into the decision: questionable calls are resolved in the opponent's favor. In other words, if you cannot make the out call promptly and with confidence, play it as in.

The standard is not “pretty sure”

A player must not call a ball out unless they can clearly see space between the line and the ball when it lands. That is a stricter and more useful test than a general feeling that the ball was long or wide.

It also explains a common court disagreement. A ball that looks out from your partner's angle may look different from yours. The governing answer is not to average the opinions or replay the point. If the team cannot agree, the ball is in.

When partners disagree

In doubles, either player may make a line call. But Rule 8.H resolves the difficult case directly: when partners disagree, there is conflict and the team's call is “in.”

That applies even if one partner saw the ball clearly out and the other thought it caught the line. The team cannot produce a unified out call, so the opponent receives the benefit of doubt.

Can you ask the opponent?

Yes—but it is a limited process, not a debate. A player may defer a line call they are responsible for making to the opponent up until the next serve is hit. If the opponent makes a clear in-or-out decision, that decision stands.

If the opponent cannot make a definitive decision, the result depends on whether you already made a call: your original call stands if you made one; if no call was made, the ball is in. After asking, you and your partner generally lose the chance to make the call yourselves, except to override in the opponent's favor.

That rule is why “ask your partner” and “ask the other team” are not interchangeable. Your partner is part of the team with the initial responsibility. The opponent may be asked only through the rule's deferral procedure.

Spectators are not a tiebreaker

Spectators must not be consulted on any call. Even a spectator standing close to the line is not a substitute for the player call, a line judge or a referee procedure.

For casual play, the cleanest recovery is simple: accept the responsible team's ruling. If a pattern of calls makes the game unworkable, address it between rallies or choose a different game format—not by turning spectators into an ad hoc officiating crew.

Prompt calls matter

An out call must be signaled promptly, by voice, hand signal or both. If you return the ball, your out call must be made before the opponent hits the next ball or before the ball becomes dead; otherwise play continues.

That prevents a player from playing a ball and then trying to revive an out call only after seeing an unfavorable result. If you do not return the ball, a prompt out call can still be recognized even if the ball becomes dead before you say it.

Referees and line judges change the procedure

In an officiated match, use the tournament's officiating structure. A line judge assigned to a line makes calls for that line; a referee may have appeal or overrule responsibilities under the tournament rules. Do not assume that the recreational-player procedure fully replaces those roles.

The core certainty standard remains useful: “out” requires a clear view. But which official gives the final ruling depends on whether the match has line judges, a referee, or both.

Why players get this wrong

Players often confuse three separate questions: where the ball landed, who is responsible for the call, and whether anyone else may be consulted. The rulebook separates them. Your team calls balls on your end; a close ball is in; partner disagreement is in; spectators are out of the process; and an opponent's opinion matters only when the responsible player formally defers to it.

Bottom line

Call only your end of the court. Call out only when you clearly see daylight between ball and line. If you or your partner are unsure, give the ball to the opponent.
