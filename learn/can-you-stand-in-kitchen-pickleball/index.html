Can You Stand in the Kitchen in Pickleball?

Yes. You may stand in the kitchen, walk through it and enter it before the ball bounces. You may hit a ball from there after it bounces. What you cannot do is volley while you—or anything in contact with you—touches the non-volley zone.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialChecked August 15, 2026
The ruling: being in the kitchen is legal. Volleying while touching it is a fault. The official name—non-volley zone—describes the restriction more accurately than the nickname.

The decision table

SituationRuling
You stand or walk in the kitchen without hitting a volleyLegal.
You enter the kitchen before your opponent's shot bouncesLegal. Entry does not have to wait for the bounce.
You hit the ball from the kitchen after it bounced anywhere on your sideLegal. The return is not a volley.
You volley while a foot touches the kitchen or its boundary lineFault.
You hit a bounced dink and remain in the kitchenLegal. You are not required to leave immediately.
You leave the kitchen, then volley before both feet establish completely outsideFault.
Your partner stands in the kitchen while you volley from outside without touching themLegal. Their position alone does not create a fault.

The rule regulates a shot, not your general position

USA Pickleball calls the kitchen the non-volley zone. Rule 11.A permits a player, or anything in contact with the player, to contact the zone at any time except during the act of volleying.

That creates a simple distinction:

  • Presence: standing, walking or waiting in the kitchen is legal.
  • Action: striking a ball before it bounces while touching the kitchen is a fault.

The casual instruction “stay out of the kitchen” may encourage useful positioning, but it is not an accurate statement of the rule.

You may enter before the ball bounces

You do not have to wait outside the line until a short ball lands. You may anticipate the bounce, step into the kitchen and wait there. If the ball then bounces before you strike it, your return is a groundstroke rather than a volley.

The timing of your entry is not the test. The test is whether you were touching the non-volley zone during the act of volleying.

The ball does not have to bounce inside the kitchen

This is the important detail many explanations miss. The ball may bounce anywhere on your side of the court. Once that shot has bounced, hitting it from inside the kitchen does not violate the non-volley-zone rule.

Example: your opponent's ball lands near your baseline, takes an unusual spin and reaches you while you are standing in the kitchen. You may legally hit it from there because the ball already bounced. The bounce location is irrelevant to the kitchen ruling.

You may stay after playing a bounced ball

After stepping in to play a dink, you are not required to exit the kitchen immediately. You may remain there while the rally continues. But your available shots are restricted: if the next ball reaches you before bouncing, you cannot volley it while you are still touching the zone.

That distinction separates legality from strategy. Remaining in the kitchen can leave you unable to attack a ball out of the air, but the position itself is not a fault.

Both feet must re-establish outside before a volley

After contacting the kitchen, you become eligible to volley again only when both feet contact the playing surface completely outside the non-volley zone. One foot outside is not enough. Neither is jumping from inside and striking the ball before landing outside.

Exit sequenceMay you volley?
One foot outside; the other still touches the kitchenNo.
Both feet airborne after jumping from insideNo.
First foot lands outside; second foot is still airborneNo.
Both feet have contacted the surface completely outsideYes, provided no other rule is violated.

Your partner may stand there too

In doubles, a partner standing in the kitchen does not make your outside volley illegal. The fault arises if the volleying player or something in contact with that player contacts the zone. It can also arise if the volleyer's momentum causes contact with a partner who is touching the kitchen.

So the correct question is not “Was either partner in the kitchen?” It is “Was the volleying player connected to the zone during the volley action or its momentum?”

The kitchen does not extend upward

Rule 3.A.4.c defines the non-volley zone as two-dimensional: it does not extend above the playing surface. A player standing outside may lean across the line and volley with the paddle over the kitchen, provided neither the player nor anything in contact with the player touches the zone.

The boundary line itself is part of the kitchen. A toe on that line during a volley is a fault even if the rest of the player remains outside.

When legal standing becomes part of a fault

Standing in the kitchen can matter in three specific ways:

  1. You volley while touching it. That is a Rule 11.A.1 fault.
  2. You have not re-established both feet outside. Volleying then is a Rule 11.A.3 fault.
  3. Your partner's volley momentum carries them into you. If you are touching the kitchen, Rule 11.A.2 makes it a fault against the volleying partner.

A separate situation runs in the other direction: if a legal volley from outside carries the volleyer into the zone, the kitchen momentum rule applies even after the ball becomes dead.

Bottom line

You may enter and stand in the kitchen whenever you want. You may hit from there after the ball bounces—wherever that bounce occurred. Before volleying again, establish both feet completely outside and keep the entire volley action clear of the zone.
