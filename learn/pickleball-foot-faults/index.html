Pickleball Foot Faults: Baseline & Kitchen Rules

A service foot fault is judged when the paddle hits the ball. At that instant, at least one foot must touch the correct serving area, neither foot may touch the court or baseline, and neither foot may touch the ground outside the imaginary sideline and centerline extensions. Kitchen violations follow a separate rule.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialChecked August 15, 2026
The ruling: behind the baseline, inside the correct serving area and with at least one foot grounded when the serve is struck. For volleys, the kitchen line counts as part of the kitchen—and volley momentum can produce a fault even after the ball is dead.

Foot-fault decisions at a glance

The legal serving area has three boundaries

Rule 3.A.4.g defines the right and left serving areas behind the baseline. Each is bounded by the imaginary extensions of the sideline and centerline for the correct service court. The imaginary boundaries are included in the serving area; a foot becomes illegal when it contacts the playing surface beyond one of them.

The baseline is different. The court lies on the other side of it, and Rule 7.A.2 does not allow either foot to contact the court when the serve is hit. A shoe touching the baseline at contact is therefore a foot fault—not a close call that receives the line.

The three service-foot requirements

USA Pickleball places server positioning in Rules 7.A.1 through 7.A.3. All three must be satisfied when the serve is hit:

  1. Grounded: at least one foot must contact the correct serving area.
  2. Outside the court: neither foot may contact the court or baseline.
  3. Inside the serving area: neither foot may contact the playing surface outside the correct area's imaginary side boundaries.

This means a legal serve does not require both feet on the ground. One grounded foot is enough. But a jump serve with both feet airborne at paddle contact is a fault.

The exact moment that matters

The rule repeatedly uses the moment when the serve is hit. A player may move before the strike and may step across the baseline after the strike. Freeze the action at paddle–ball contact: that is the frame used to judge the feet.

The same positioning rules govern both volley serves and drop serves. A drop serve can bounce anywhere on the playing surface before it is hit, but the server's feet still must satisfy Rule 7.A at paddle contact. The ball's bounce location does not relocate the legal serving area.

Who can call a service foot fault?

In player-called play, players are expected to call faults on themselves or their partner. Rule 9.B.3 also expressly permits players to call a service foot fault on an opponent. The call should be made as soon as the fault is committed or detected and no later than before the next serve is hit.

If the teams disagree about that player call, the 2026 rule requires a replay. If partners disagree, the decision goes in favor of their opponents. This procedure matters: an opponent may enforce a service foot fault, but not every suspected service-motion violation carries the same player-call authority.

In an officiated match, the referee is responsible for service foot faults. When line judges are assigned, they make foot-fault calls for their assigned lines. If the referee is uncertain about the server's foot contact with or outside the serving area, Rule 20.E.2 allows a replay before the return of serve.

A “kitchen foot fault” is a different rule

Players often use one label for two different violations. Section 7 controls service foot faults. Section 11 controls contact with the non-volley zone during a volley.

The kitchen and every line that bounds it are part of the non-volley zone. It is a fault when a volleying player—or anything in contact with that player—touches the zone. It is also a fault when momentum from the volley causes prohibited contact, even after the ball becomes dead.

Standing in the kitchen is otherwise legal. A player may enter the zone before a bounce, wait there and hit the ball after it bounces. After touching the zone, however, the player must put both feet completely outside on the playing surface before volleying again.

Common calls, resolved

The server's toe hangs over the baseline but does not touch it

Legal under the foot-position rule. The rule concerns contact. A shoe may extend above the court without touching it, just as the non-volley zone is two-dimensional and does not extend upward.

The server drags the back foot across the centerline extension after contact

Legal as a service-foot matter if the foot was still in the correct serving area when the ball was struck. Later movement does not retroactively change the serve.

The drop-serve ball bounces inside the court

That bounce location is legal. Rule 7.D.4 places no restriction on where the drop-serve ball bounces. The server's feet—not the bounce—must remain compliant when the ball is hit.

A player volleys while a heel touches the kitchen line

Fault. The line is part of the non-volley zone, and partial foot contact is enough.

Wheelchair play uses wheel-contact rules

For wheelchair players, Rule 25.A.8 modifies the serving-position test. Both large rear wheels must be on the playing surface behind the baseline and must not touch outside the correct serving area; the smaller front wheels may extend into the court. The wheelchair is treated as part of the player's body elsewhere in the adaptive rules.

Bottom line

For the serve, watch the feet at paddle contact: one must be grounded in the correct serving area, neither may touch the court, and neither may touch beyond the imaginary side boundaries. For the kitchen, ask a different question: was the player volleying, still carried by volley momentum, or not yet fully re-established outside?
