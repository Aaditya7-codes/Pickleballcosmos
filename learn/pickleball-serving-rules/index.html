Pickleball Serving Rules 2026: What Makes a Serve Legal

There are two legal ways to serve: the volley serve and the drop serve. They share rules on position, release and placement, but the familiar upward-arc, paddle-head and waist restrictions apply specifically to the volley serve.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 15, 2026
The short version: serve from the correct area behind the baseline, send the ball diagonally into the correct service court, and clear the non-volley zone. For a volley serve, the paddle must travel in a clear upward arc, the paddle head must clearly not be above the wrist joint, and the ball must clearly be no higher than the server's waist at contact. A legal drop serve is not subject to those three volley-serve motion restrictions.

1. Start in the correct serving area

At the moment the ball is served, the server must be positioned behind the baseline and between the imaginary extensions of the sideline and centerline for the correct service court. At least one foot must be in contact with the playing surface or ground behind the baseline. A foot touching the baseline or court at contact is a service foot fault.

The practical version is simple: stand behind the baseline, do not step on it, and stay between the extended centerline and sideline when you strike the serve.

2. The release cannot be used to pre-spin the ball

USA Pickleball's 2026 Rule 7.B.2 separates spin on release from spin created by the paddle at contact. Natural rotation during the release is expected, but the server must not manipulate the ball to add spin with the body or paddle before striking it. The rule permits the ball to roll off the paddle face by gravity.

Spin generated when the paddle actually hits the ball is legal. “Spin serves are illegal” is therefore too broad: pre-spin on the release is prohibited; paddle-generated spin at contact is not.

3. A volley serve has three contact requirements

A volley serve is struck before the ball bounces. In 2026, USA Pickleball strengthened the wording of the three motion/contact requirements by adding the idea that each must be clear.

The paddle must move in a clear upward arc

Rule 7.C.1 requires the paddle to be moving in a clear upward arc when it contacts the ball.

The paddle head must clearly not be above the wrist joint

Under Rule 7.C.2, the highest point of the paddle head must clearly not be above the highest part of the server's wrist joint at ball contact.

The ball must clearly be no higher than the waist

Rule 7.C.3 says the ball must clearly be no higher than the server's waist when the paddle contacts it. The 2026 rule uses the word waist.

Where exactly is the “waist” in the 2026 serve rule?

The 2026 USA Pickleball rulebook does not give “waist” a more precise anatomical definition. That matters because some current explainers turn the rule into “below the navel” as though USA Pickleball itself defined the waist that way. It does not. Rule 7.C.3 says waist, and that is the term that controls.

The ambiguity is real enough that a suggested 2027 rule change, proposal #2703, was filed specifically to define “waist.” The proposal would add a definition based on the abdomen between the rib cage and hips. Its reasoning says players currently use competing definitions, including the belly button, because the rulebook does not define the term.

That proposed definition is not the 2026 rule. A proposal for the next rulebook should not be back-dated into the current one.

Practical ruling: if you want a volley serve that is clearly legal rather than borderline, make contact comfortably below any debatable waist interpretation. Or use the drop serve, which is not subject to the volley serve's waist-height, upward-arc or paddle-head requirements.

4. The drop serve follows a different motion rule

For a drop serve, the server releases the ball, allows it to bounce, and then strikes it. The volley-serve requirements concerning upward arc, paddle-head position and contact height do not apply to the drop serve.

The ball must be released rather than propelled to manufacture the bounce. The release must still comply with the rule against adding spin before paddle contact. Foot-position and service-placement rules also still apply.

Useful distinction: a drop serve is not simply a volley serve after a bounce. Its legality is judged differently.

5. The serve must go diagonally into the correct service court

Under Rule 7.E, the serve goes to the service court diagonally opposite the server's correct position. It must clear the opponent's non-volley zone and land in the correct service court.

The centerline, sideline and baseline count as in. The non-volley-zone line is different: on a serve, a ball touching that line is short and therefore a fault.

6. A serve may touch the net and still be legal

There is no tennis-style service let merely because the ball clips the net. The 2026 rule expressly allows the served ball to clear the non-volley zone “with or without touching the net.” If it touches the net and then lands in the correct service court, play continues.

If it touches the net and lands short, on the non-volley-zone line, or outside the correct service court, the serve is a fault.

7. There is no tennis-style second serve

A server does not receive a second attempt after committing a service fault. In doubles, losing the serve may pass service to the partner or produce a side out depending on which server is serving. In singles, a service fault produces a side out.

Where do you serve from?

In singles, the server serves from the right side when the server's score is even and from the left when it is odd.

In doubles, the first serve after a side out begins from the right-hand court. The serving player switches sides after scoring a point and continues serving until the serving team loses the rally. The partner then serves when applicable. The opening service sequence is the exception: the starting team begins with only one server before the first side out, which is why traditional doubles starts at 0–0–2.

What actually changed for 2026?

  • Volley-serve language became stricter: Rule 7.C now requires a clear upward arc, clearly compliant paddle-head position and clearly compliant ball height.
  • Spin was clarified: Rule 7.B.2 makes explicit that spin may be created by the paddle at contact even though the server may not manipulate the ball to add spin during the release.
  • Serve placement was clarified: Rule 7.E expressly says the ball must land in the correct diagonal service court and may clear the non-volley zone with or without touching the net.
  • The rulebook was reorganized: serving is now grouped in Section 7.

Quick answers

Does a pickleball serve have to be underhand?

For the traditional volley serve, the rule is expressed through the required clear upward paddle arc, paddle-head position and waist-height restriction. A drop serve is not subject to those three volley-serve motion requirements.

Where is the waist for a legal pickleball serve?

The 2026 rule says “waist” but does not define it more precisely. Do not treat “navel” as official 2026 wording. A suggested 2027 change would define the term, but it is not current law.

Can a pickleball serve hit the net?

Yes. If it clears the non-volley zone and lands in the correct diagonal service court, it is legal.

Can you add spin to a pickleball serve?

Yes, with the paddle at contact. You may not manipulate the release to pre-spin the ball.

Does the serve have to land past the kitchen line?

Yes. A serve touching the non-volley-zone line is short and a fault.

Can you serve overhand on a drop serve?

The three volley-serve restrictions do not apply to the drop serve, but the drop itself and the rest of the service rules still apply. For a sanctioned rules dispute, use the current rulebook rather than reducing the drop serve to “overhand” or “underhand.”

A note on recreational versus officiated play

The underlying service rules are the same, but enforcement procedures are not identical in every setting. For sanctioned competition or a disputed borderline serve, consult the current rulebook and event officiating procedures.
