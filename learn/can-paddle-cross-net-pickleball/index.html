Can You Hit a Pickleball Before It Crosses the Net?

No. The ball must entirely cross the plane of the net to your end before you hit it. And even after the ball crosses, your paddle or body may not cross the plane before contact. Crossing is allowed only immediately after a legal hit, as part of that strike’s continuation.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 15, 2026
The ruling: first, the ball must entirely cross to your end. Second, the player, paddle and anything worn or carried must stay on that side until the hit. After a legal hit, crossing may continue only as part of that same strike.

The rule is about the plane of the net

The key concept is the plane of the net: imagine a vertical sheet extending upward from the net and continuing beyond the net posts. The 2026 USA Pickleball rulebook groups these situations in Section 13, including rules for a ball crossing the net plane and for a player crossing it.

The practical rule is not “your paddle can never go over the net.” The real question is when it crosses.

SituationRuling

1. The ball must cross first

In normal play, the ball must first cross the plane of the net to your side before you strike it. Reaching across and making contact while the ball is still on the opponent’s side is a fault.

This is the part that matters most during fast exchanges at the kitchen line. A paddle can be physically above the net after the stroke; the illegal act is making contact too early, before the ball has crossed the plane.

2. Your paddle cannot cross first, either

These are separate limits. A ball may have crossed to your side, but it is still a fault if any part of you—or anything you wear or carry, including the paddle—crosses the plane before you hit the ball.

The legal sequence is: ball crosses, you strike it from your side, then your follow-through may cross.

3. Your paddle can cross after legal contact

Once you have legally struck the ball, the rules allow the player and anything the player is wearing or carrying to cross the plane immediately afterward, but only while executing that current strike and its continuation. That includes the paddle.

So a natural follow-through that carries the paddle over the net is not automatically a fault. The same principle can apply to a player’s hand, arm or upper body.

Think contact first, follow-through second: if the ball crossed to your side before you hit it, the paddle crossing the plane afterward can be legal. If the paddle crosses so that you hit the ball while it is still on the opponent’s side, it is not.

4. Crossing the plane does not let you touch the net

The permission to cross the plane after the shot is not permission to touch everything on the other side. While the ball remains live, touching the net system is a fault. Contacting the opponent or the opponent’s court can also create a fault under the applicable net rules.

This distinction matters on aggressive volleys and Erne-style plays: the paddle may cross the imaginary plane, but the net itself remains off-limits while the ball is live.

5. The backspin exception

There is one unusual situation in which a player may intentionally reach across the plane to strike a ball on the opponent’s side.

If the ball first bounces in your court and then, because of backspin, wind or another permitted effect, returns to the opponent’s side without being hit, you are allowed to cross the plane of the net to play it. The ball must first have crossed back to the opponent’s side before you cross the plane.

USA Pickleball considered removing the ability to reach under the net in this exceptional situation for 2026, but the proposal was disapproved. The governing body specifically noted that the existing plane-of-net rules already regulate the shot and that some legal net configurations leave space below the net.

6. What about an Erne?

An Erne does not give a player special permission to hit the ball early. The ball still needs to cross the plane before contact. What makes the shot possible is the player’s position outside the sideline near the net, not an exemption from the plane-of-net rule.

After the legal hit, the paddle or body may cross the plane as part of the follow-through, subject to the restrictions on touching the net, opponent or opponent’s court while the ball remains live.

Quick answers

Can my paddle go over the net after I hit the ball?

Yes. A follow-through may carry the paddle across the plane after legal contact.

Can I reach over the net to volley?

Not in ordinary play. The ball must cross to your side before contact, and your paddle cannot cross the plane before that contact.

Can my hand cross the net?

It can cross the plane after legal contact, subject to the same restrictions on touching the net, opponent or opponent’s court while the ball is live.

Can I touch the net after hitting the ball?

Not while the ball is still live. Crossing the plane is different from touching the net system.

Can I hit a ball that has spun back over the net?

Yes. If the ball first bounced on your side and then returned to the opponent’s side, you may cross the plane to play it after it has crossed back, provided you comply with the remaining net-contact rules.

Who makes the call?

In player-called play, a player is expected to call a net-plane fault on themselves or their partner. An opponent may point out the issue after the rally, but may enforce only non-volley-zone and service-foot faults against an opponent. In an officiated match, the referee calls rule violations.
