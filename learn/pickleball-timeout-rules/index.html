Pickleball time-out rules

In standard USA Pickleball play, a singles player or doubles team gets two one-minute time-outs in an 11- or 15-point game, and three in a 21-point game. The request must come before the next serve.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialReviewed August 27, 2026
The practical rule: say or signal “time-out” between rallies, before the next serve. A request after the serve has been hit is a fault. Do not confuse a standard time-out with a medical, equipment or referee interruption.

That allowance belongs to the individual in singles and to the team in doubles. A doubles pair does not receive two separate banks of time-outs.

When you can call one

A standard time-out may be requested between rallies, before the next serve occurs, or between games. The request needs to be audible by voice, visible by hand signal, or both, and directed to the opposing player or team and, in officiated play, the referee.

The clean court habit is simple: call it clearly as the rally ends. Walking away for water without communicating is not the same thing as a clearly requested time-out.

A late time-out request is a fault

The timing line is strict. Under Rule 21.A.2.a, a player who calls a standard time-out after the serve has been hit commits a fault. A time-out is not a way to stop a live ball or undo a difficult return.

That is different from a valid safety interruption or a referee stopping play for an extenuating circumstance. Those situations use different rules and should not be relabeled as a routine tactical time-out.

Can play resume early?

Yes. One minute is a maximum, not a compulsory wait. If all players are ready, play may resume before the full period expires. In officiated play, the referee manages the restart and score call.

Standard, medical, referee and equipment time-outs are not interchangeable

TypePurposeWhat to know
StandardTactical break, water, regroupingLimited by game format; up to one minute; must be called before the next serve.
MedicalMedical attention during a matchUses a separate 15-minute framework and referee/medical procedure. It is not a free extended standard break.
RefereeExtenuating circumstancesCalled by the referee; there is no fixed time limit because the interruption depends on the circumstance.
EquipmentEquipment or apparel issue affecting fair, safe playHandled under its own procedure; an equipment issue is not automatically charged as a standard time-out.

Medical time-outs need more care than a headline answer

A player needing medical attention should request a medical time-out from the referee. In sanctioned play, medical personnel—or the tournament director if medical personnel are unavailable—assess the situation, and the 15-minute period begins when they arrive.

The full medical framework includes validity, rescinding a request, treatment and retirement provisions. It is not sensible to turn a possible injury into a tactics question. In a sanctioned match, follow the referee and medical procedure; in a recreational game, stop play and handle a genuine safety concern before worrying about the score.

What about local recreational play?

Many public-court games adopt a looser rhythm, especially when there is no referee, no formal game format or a local rotation system. That does not create a universal alternative rule. Before a league or event starts, check its published format. For a USA Pickleball-sanctioned match, the current official rulebook controls.

Quick answers

Does each doubles player get two time-outs?

No. In an 11- or 15-point game, the doubles team gets two standard time-outs total.

Can you call a time-out before a game starts?

No. The standard-time-out rule does not permit one before the match begins.

Can you call a time-out when you are receiving?

Yes, if it is between rallies and before the next serve. The rule is not limited to the serving side.

Can you call one after the opponent serves?

No. Once the serve has been hit, a standard time-out request is late and is a fault against the caller.

Does a water break automatically count as a time-out?

Do not assume it does or does not. A standard time-out must be clearly requested. In officiated play, the referee controls the applicable procedure.

Bottom line

Call a standard time-out clearly, between rallies and before the serve. You get two per player or doubles team in games to 11 or 15, and three in games to 21; each may last up to one minute. Medical, referee and equipment interruptions follow different rules.
