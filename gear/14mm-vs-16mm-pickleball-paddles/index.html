14mm vs 16mm Pickleball Paddles: What Thickness Actually Tells You

A 16mm label does not automatically mean “control,” and 14mm does not automatically mean “power.” Thickness is one construction variable inside a system that also includes shape, total mass, mass distribution, face, core architecture and manufacturing method.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 11, 2026
The practical answer: if two paddles are genuinely comparable in every other important respect, core thickness may help explain differences in feel. But across different models, thickness alone is too weak a signal to predict which paddle will be faster, more stable, more powerful or easier to control.

What “14mm” and “16mm” actually describe

In the modern paddle market, 14mm and 16mm usually refer to the nominal depth of the paddle construction or core family. USA Pickleball's approved-equipment records show both thicknesses across many legal paddles, including model families offered in more than one depth.

That matters because neither number is itself a quality grade or a rules category. A paddle can be USA Pickleball approved at 14mm, 16mm or another depth, provided the submitted model satisfies the applicable equipment standards.

The approved list also shows why thickness should not be treated as a proxy for construction. Current records include 14mm and 16mm paddles built with polypropylene honeycomb, EPP foam and other architectures, along with different face materials and shapes.

The shortcut most buyers should avoid

14mm = powerA thinner nominal profile does not tell you the paddle's swing weight, face stiffness, core architecture, perimeter weighting or rebound characteristics.
16mm = controlA thicker paddle can still be head-heavy, stiff or highly power-oriented depending on the rest of the construction.

16mm = more stableOff-center stability depends on more than depth. Width and mass distribution materially affect resistance to twisting.

Thickness becomes useful when the comparison is controlled

The cleanest way to learn what thickness does is to compare two versions of the same paddle family, ideally with the same shape, face technology and broadly similar weight specification.

USA Pickleball's current approved-equipment database contains many families with both 14mm and 16mm variants. For example, Engage lists Axiom Elongated versions in both depths, and the database has long included paired 14mm/16mm models from brands such as CRBN and 11SIX24. Franklin's C45 Aurelius family is listed in 12.7mm, 14mm and 16mm versions.

Those paired products are more informative than comparing an unrelated 14mm elongated paddle with a widebody 16mm paddle and attributing every difference to two millimeters of depth.

Five specifications to check before thickness

1. Shape and width

An elongated paddle and a widebody paddle distribute their hitting area differently. Width also matters to off-center stability. If the shapes differ materially, thickness is no longer the only meaningful variable.

2. Static weight

Static weight is the number you see on a scale. It matters, but it does not tell you where that mass sits. Manufacturer weight ranges can also be broad enough that two retail examples of the same model are not identical.

3. Swing weight

Swing weight describes resistance to rotational acceleration around a defined axis. In practical terms, it helps explain why two paddles with similar scale weight can feel different when you swing or reposition them.

A 14mm paddle is therefore not automatically the quicker paddle. If its mass distribution produces a higher swing weight than a particular 16mm model, the simple thickness shortcut can point in the wrong direction.

4. Twist weight

Twist weight concerns resistance to rotation from an off-center impact. It is useful when thinking about lateral stability and how much the paddle wants to twist when contact misses the centerline.

Again, thickness alone cannot substitute for the measurement. Paddle width and where mass is positioned around the head can change the result.

5. Construction and face

Polypropylene honeycomb, foam structures, perimeter foam, thermoforming, face layup and bonding methods can all differ between paddles that share the same nominal thickness. A “16mm carbon paddle” is therefore not a sufficiently precise description of how a product is built.

So what tends to change with thickness?

There are reasonable engineering reasons for thickness to influence how a paddle flexes and responds, but a consumer should be careful about turning that into a universal ranking. In a controlled design, changing the depth can alter structural behavior. In the market, however, manufacturers commonly change other variables at the same time.

That is why Pickleball Cosmos does not publish a universal claim such as “16mm is X% more stable” or “14mm generates Y% more power.” Without a controlled test across matched constructions, those numbers would be false precision.

Which thickness should a beginner choose?

For a first paddle, thickness should be a secondary filter, not the starting point.

How to compare a 14mm and 16mm version of the same paddle

  1. Confirm they really are the same family. Similar names do not guarantee identical face, core or manufacturing details.
  2. Compare dimensions. Make sure shape, length and width are not changing at the same time.
  3. Compare the actual weight range. Do not assume the thinner version is lighter.
  4. Look for credible swing- and twist-weight measurements. Record who measured them and under what method.
  5. Check current approval status. Approval belongs to the exact model/configuration, not to a brand name in the abstract.
  6. Only then evaluate feel claims. Manufacturer language can be useful context, but it is still a manufacturer claim unless independently measured or physically tested.

Does 16mm mean a bigger sweet spot?

Not automatically. “Sweet spot” itself is often used loosely in marketing. A wider face, perimeter mass and structural design can all affect how forgiving off-center contact feels. Nominal core depth is only one input.

Does 14mm mean more spin?

No reliable universal rule follows from thickness alone. Spin depends on the paddle-ball interaction, including surface characteristics, face response, incoming ball conditions and stroke. USA Pickleball separately regulates equipment performance and surface characteristics; a thickness label is not a spin rating.

Does one thickness last longer?

There is no defensible universal durability winner based solely on 14mm versus 16mm. Core material, bonding, edge construction, manufacturing consistency, use conditions and the specific failure mode matter more than the label.

Bottom line

Buy the paddle, not the millimeter number.

A 14mm or 16mm specification is useful context, particularly when comparing closely related versions of the same design. It is not enough to predict power, control, hand speed, stability, spin or durability across unrelated paddles.

If two candidates are otherwise close, thickness can help break the tie. If they are different in shape, mass distribution, core architecture or face construction, start with those differences first.
