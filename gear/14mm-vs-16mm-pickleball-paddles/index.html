14mm vs 16mm Pickleball Paddles: What Thickness Actually Tells You

A 16mm label does not automatically mean “control,” and 14mm does not automatically mean “power.” Thickness is one construction variable alongside shape, total mass, mass distribution, face, core architecture and manufacturing method.

By Pickleball Cosmos EditorialUpdated August 12, 2026
Use thickness as context: when two paddles are otherwise closely matched, core depth may help explain a difference in feel. Across unrelated models, it is too weak a signal to predict speed, stability, power or control on its own.

What “14mm” and “16mm” actually describe

In the modern paddle market, 14mm and 16mm usually refer to the nominal depth of the paddle construction or core family. USA Pickleball's approved-equipment records show both thicknesses across many legal paddles, including model families offered in more than one depth.

Neither number is a quality grade or rules category. A paddle can be approved at 14mm, 16mm or another depth if the submitted model satisfies the applicable equipment standards.

The approved list also shows why thickness is a poor proxy for construction: current records include 14mm and 16mm paddles built with polypropylene honeycomb, EPP foam and other architectures, with different face materials and shapes.

The shortcut most buyers should avoid

Common shortcutWhy it is incomplete
14mm = powerA thinner nominal profile does not tell you swing weight, face stiffness, core architecture, perimeter weighting or rebound characteristics.
16mm = controlA thicker paddle can still be head-heavy, stiff or highly power-oriented depending on the rest of the construction.
14mm = faster handsHand speed is strongly affected by mass distribution. Equal static weights can feel very different in motion.
16mm = more stableOff-center stability depends on more than depth. Width and mass distribution materially affect resistance to twisting.
Carbon + 16mm = premiumMaterial labels and thickness do not establish manufacturing quality, durability, approval status or fit for a particular player.

Thickness is most useful in a controlled comparison

The cleanest comparison is between two versions of the same paddle family, ideally with the same shape, face technology and broadly similar weight specification.

USA Pickleball's approved-equipment database contains families with both 14mm and 16mm variants. Engage lists Axiom Elongated versions in both depths, and the database has included paired 14mm/16mm models from brands such as CRBN and 11SIX24. Franklin's C45 Aurelius family is listed in 12.7mm, 14mm and 16mm versions.

Those pairs are more informative than comparing an unrelated 14mm elongated paddle with a widebody 16mm paddle and assigning every difference to two millimeters of depth.

Five specifications to check before thickness

1. Shape and width

An elongated paddle and a widebody paddle distribute their hitting area differently. Width also matters to off-center stability. If the shapes differ materially, thickness is no longer the only meaningful variable.

2. Static weight

Static weight is what the paddle weighs on a scale. It matters, but it does not tell you where that mass sits. Manufacturer weight ranges can also be broad enough that two retail examples of the same model are not identical.

3. Swing weight

Swing weight describes resistance to rotational acceleration around a defined axis. It helps explain why two paddles with similar scale weight can feel different when swung or repositioned.

A 14mm paddle is therefore not automatically the quicker paddle. If its mass distribution produces a higher swing weight than a particular 16mm model, the thickness shortcut points in the wrong direction.

4. Twist weight

Twist weight concerns resistance to rotation from an off-center impact. It adds context when comparing lateral stability.

Thickness cannot substitute for the measurement. Paddle width and where mass is positioned around the head can change the result.

5. Construction and face

Polypropylene honeycomb, foam structures, perimeter foam, thermoforming, face layup and bonding methods can all differ between paddles that share the same nominal thickness. A “16mm carbon paddle” is not a sufficiently precise description of how a product is built.

What tends to change with thickness?

There are sound engineering reasons for thickness to influence how a paddle flexes and responds, but that does not produce a universal ranking across the market. In a controlled design, changing depth can alter structural behavior. Manufacturers, however, often change several variables at once.

For that reason, Pickleball Cosmos does not publish universal claims such as “16mm is X% more stable” or “14mm generates Y% more power” without controlled testing of matched constructions.

How to compare two thicknesses in the same paddle family

  1. Confirm the construction is genuinely comparable. Similar names do not guarantee identical face, core or manufacturing details.
  2. Compare dimensions. Make sure shape, length and width are not changing at the same time.
  3. Compare the actual weight range. Do not assume the thinner version is lighter.
  4. Look for credible swing- and twist-weight measurements. Record who measured them and under what method.
  5. Check current approval status. Approval belongs to the exact model/configuration.
  6. Then evaluate feel claims. Manufacturer language is useful context, but it remains a manufacturer claim unless independently measured or physically tested.

Does 16mm mean a bigger sweet spot?

Not automatically. “Sweet spot” is often used loosely in marketing. Face width, perimeter mass and structural design can all affect how forgiving off-center contact feels. Nominal core depth is only one input.

Does 14mm mean more spin?

No reliable universal rule follows from thickness alone. Spin depends on the paddle-ball interaction, including surface characteristics, face response, incoming ball conditions and stroke. A thickness label is not a spin rating.

Does one thickness last longer?

There is no defensible universal durability winner based solely on 14mm versus 16mm. Core material, bonding, edge construction, manufacturing consistency, use conditions and the specific failure mode matter more than the label.

The decision

A 14mm or 16mm specification is useful context, especially when comparing closely related versions of the same design. It is not enough to predict power, control, hand speed, stability, spin or durability across unrelated paddles.

If two candidates are otherwise close, thickness can help break the tie. If they differ in shape, mass distribution, core architecture or face construction, start with those differences first.
